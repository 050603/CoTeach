## Teaching accuracy and continuity

- Explain why the learning matters when learners need that orientation, in a natural sentence tied to the task. Do not recite formal objectives or add a ceremonial course introduction.
- A concrete case must show the actions, observations, wording, or results that support the judgment. Establish what happened and why it matters before naming unfamiliar categories. A setting label alone is not a worked case.
- Give adjacent pages different learning contributions. Reusing material is useful only when the later page adds a new inference, changes one explicit condition, or asks for independent application. Do not repeat the same classification, reasons, and conclusion in new wording.
- When a case is reused, preserve its exact key sentences, step names, facts, and technical terms. State whether a later wording is an explanation or a changed condition. In a variation, name what changed and preserve every other relevant condition.
- Distinguish a definition, a feature of the current example, and a heuristic that may help judgment. Replacement, deletion, scope-of-effect, and comparison can clarify a distinction, but they are not sufficient definitions or universal decision rules. Return the conclusion to the expression's main function and applicable conditions.
- Keep the section's core concepts distinct from related design variables. A goal, constraint, resource, or observed response does not silently replace one of the concepts being taught or become a new peer category unless the adopted source explicitly defines that relationship.
- A preserved condition means only that the current comparison holds it constant. Describe it as a condition of this example, never as something that generally cannot be changed. Requiring new justification for a change is not the same as prohibiting the change.
- Support classifications with positive evidence and the relationship that makes the conclusion follow. The absence of an ordered sequence, the presence of several named steps, a document heading, or a keyword is not sufficient by itself.
- Before asking learners to compare or judge a case, provide the specific goal, actions, and observed or intended result that the judgment depends on. Clearly distinguish an observation from a prediction, expectation, or hypothetical outcome.
- When one concept is described as a concretization of another, explain what principle or relationship is preserved, what functional activity arrangement it becomes, how those activities depend on one another, and how that organization supports learning. A sequence of stage names alone does not establish the relationship.
- When explaining relative stability, identify what remains stable, what may vary, and why the stable part matters. When using a concrete utterance or action as an example of a method, explain how that interaction changes learners' attention, reasoning, or operation in support of the stated goal; the utterance itself is only an instance.
- Absence of an explicit rationale, condition, or fact in a written plan establishes only that it was not stated there. It does not prove that the underlying design, condition, or fact does not exist.
- Source quotations constrain what the source says. Do not treat an accurate quotation as evidence for an additional inference unless the source or established domain knowledge supports that inference.
